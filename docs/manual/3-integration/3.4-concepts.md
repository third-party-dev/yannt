## 3.4 Conceptual model - how the tool things about its domain

<!-- TODO

The conceptual model in the integration guide is written from the outside looking in. It describes the library in terms of the objects and operations the developer interacts with — the public surface. The goal is to give an integrating developer the mental model they need to use the library correctly and predictably. Internal mechanisms are only mentioned if they affect observable behavior.

**Introduce terms through a narrative of what the tool actually does** - Walk through what happens when the library is invoked — not as a tutorial, but as a description of the process. "When you call X, the library first does A, producing a B. That B is then passed to C, which..." This lets you introduce each term at the moment it's needed, so the definition has an immediate referent. Engineers follow cause-and-effect well.
**Use the diagram as an anchor, not a summary** - Rather than a diagram that tries to show everything upfront, consider one that maps directly to the narrative — so as the reader works through the text, the diagram is something they keep glancing back at. Labels in the diagram should use exactly the terms you define in the text, no synonyms.
**Define terms at the point of first use, then be consistent** - For a detail-oriented engineer, precise terminology matters. Define a term once, clearly, and then use it exactly that way every time. The definition can be a sentence or two inline, not a bullet. If you do include a glossary or definition list, put it after the narrative, as a reference — not before, as a prerequisite.
**Capture relationships explicitly, not just components** - This is often the gap in conceptual docs. Engineers can infer what individual components do, but the relationships are where the design decisions live — what owns what, what transforms what, what is stateful vs stateless, what's 1:1 vs 1:many. A short table or even just explicit prose ("a Foo contains one or more Bars, but a Bar has no reference back to its parent Foo") is worth a lot.
**Use the "open loop" technique you mentioned for non-obvious design choices** - Where the model might surprise someone — or where a reasonable person might ask "why isn't this just X?" — a short discursive paragraph is exactly right. This is where you explain the reasoning behind the model, not just the model itself. Engineers respect that more than a bare definition because it treats them as someone who will eventually need to work with edge cases.

The mix you're describing — narrative, inline definitions, relational callouts, and open-loop explanation for the non-obvious bits — is actually the right shape. The main discipline is sequencing: earn each term before defining it, and don't front-load the reader with structure before they have any intuition for what the structure describes. -->
